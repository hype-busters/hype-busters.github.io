Epistemic Stance Survey — Design & Recruitment

How the study is built and how many participants it needs. This document explains what we are measuring, the question format, the five-survey split, and — step by step, in plain terms — exactly how we arrive at the minimum number of paid annotators.

What We Measure

We rank English words and phrases by how strongly they signal an epistemic stance — how certain, evidence-based, or committed a claim sounds. "This proves" is a far stronger stance than "this might suggest".
Why Best-Worst Scaling, not a rating scale Asking people to rate each word on a 1–7 scale is noisy — everyone uses the scale differently. Instead we show a few words in context and ask which is strongest and which is weakest. This forces a direct comparison, is easier to answer, and yields cleaner, more reliable rankings from fewer judgements.

Words are grouped into five surveys, one per stance category, so participants only compare words that genuinely belong together.

The Question Format

Each question is a block: four sentences that differ only by the stance word. The participant picks the strongest and the weakest of the four.
Why four items per block is efficient Picking a best and a worst from four options settles 5 of the 6 possible pairwise comparisons in that block at once. That is what makes BWS so economical — a handful of blocks implies a large number of comparisons.
v
distinct items (words) in a survey
k = 4
items shown per block (per question)
b
number of blocks (questions) in the survey
r
times each item is seen by one participant

These four numbers are tied together by a simple accounting identity: the total item-slots shown is b × k, and when every item appears equally that equals v × r. So b × 4 = v × r.

The Five Surveys

Surveys 3–5 use a strict BIBD (Balanced Incomplete Block Design) — every pair of words is compared exactly once, the gold standard. Surveys 1–2 have too many words for a strict design to stay cheap, so they use a near-balanced design that still connects every word into one comparison network.

How Many Annotators — Step by Step

This is the part that costs money, so it is worth going slowly. The goal is to collect just enough judgements for each word's score to be stable — and not one paid response more.
  • 1

    Decide how precise each word's score must be

    Every word ends up with a score (roughly, how often it was picked as strongest). Because we only sample a limited number of people, that score carries a margin of error — exactly like a poll saying "52% ± 3%". That "±" part is the half-width, written h. Smaller h = more precise = more people.

  • 2

    Use the polling formula for observations

    Like any poll, the margin of error shrinks with the square root of the number of observations M collected for a word. Using a conservative (unit) variability per judgement — a deliberately safe over-estimate — and 95% confidence gives the standard result below, which we then rearrange to solve for the observations we need.

  • 3

    Convert observations into people

    The money-saving insight: one participant does not give one observation per word — they give r of them, because they see each word r times. So the number of people is the observations divided by r. A design that shows each word more often needs fewer people.

Margin of error from M observations
h = 1.96 / √M
1.96 is the 95%-confidence multiplier.
Rearranged — observations, then people
M = (1.96 / h)²   →   N = M / r
More precision (smaller h) → more people. More exposures (bigger r) → fewer people.
In plain words That is why S3 needs the fewest annotators despite having the most words: each participant sees every S3 word 9 times (r = 9), so each person contributes a lot of comparisons.

Why these are a floor, not a guess The formula assumes conservative (unit) variability and 95% confidence, so real precision is usually a little better than promised. We treat these numbers as a minimum and confirm with the reliability checks below, rather than paying for extra people up front.

Cost-Aware Recruitment Plan

Rather than committing to the full "strong" numbers immediately, we start lean and top up only the surveys that need it. Most surveys stabilise early, so this minimises spend.
Wave 1 targets S1 = 50 · S2 = 50 · S3 = 30 · S4 = 40 · S5 = 50 participants. Run the checks below, then add +15 only to surveys that fail — repeat until each passes twice in a row, then stop paying for that survey.
Split-half reliability ≥ 0.75Prefer 0.80+. Split responses into two random halves; the two rankings should strongly agree.
Quartile overlap ≥ 85%The strongest and weakest words stay the same as more data arrives.

Pipeline From Here

Seven steps from the current frozen design to final published scores.
  • 1

    Freeze the design

    Tag the current S1–S5 CSVs as design_v1 so item lists and blocks cannot drift mid-study.

  • 2

    Confirm data capture

    Verify the live Apps Script logs every column: Form ID, Block ID, Survey Number, choices, and participant metadata.

  • 3

    Pilot QA — 5–10 per survey

    Check rows land in the sheet, block counts match, and sentences render correctly.

  • 4

    Run Wave 1

    Recruit the lean targets above (S1=50, S2=50, S3=30, S4=40, S5=50).

  • 5

    Checkpoint every +15

    Recompute split-half reliability and rank stability per survey.

  • 6

    Selective top-up

    Add participants only to surveys still failing — usually S1/S2 first, since they are near-balanced.

  • 7

    Finalise scores

    Produce BW-normalised scores plus a model-based estimate, and report each word's score with its uncertainty.
