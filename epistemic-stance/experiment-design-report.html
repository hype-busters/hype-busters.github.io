0 What this experiment measures

We want to rank English words and phrases by how strongly they signal an epistemic stance — how certain, evidence-based, or committed a claim sounds. For example, "this proves" is a much stronger stance than "this might suggest".

Instead of asking people to rate each word on a 1–7 scale (which people use inconsistently), we show a few words in context and ask them to pick the strongest and the weakest. This is called Best-Worst Scaling (BWS), and it gives cleaner, more reliable rankings from fewer judgements.

Words are grouped into five surveys, one per stance category, so people only compare words that belong together.

1 The question format

One question = one block

Each question shows 4 sentences that differ only by the stance word. The participant picks the strongest and the weakest of the four. Every "pick strongest + weakest" out of 4 implies 5 of the 6 possible pairwise comparisons at once — that is why BWS is so efficient.

v
number of distinct items (words) in a survey
k = 4
items shown per block (per question)
b
number of blocks (questions) in the survey
r
how many times each item is seen by one participant

These four numbers are linked by a simple accounting identity: total item-slots shown is b × k, and if every item is shown equally that equals v × r. So b × 4 = v × r.

2 The current survey split

Surveys 3–5 use a strict BIBD (Balanced Incomplete Block Design): every pair of words is compared exactly once, which is the gold standard. Surveys 1–2 have too many words for a strict design to be cheap, so they use a near-balanced design that still connects every word into one comparison network.

"Fully connected" means there are no isolated words — every word can be ranked relative to every other word, even in S1/S2 where not all pairs are directly compared. Indirect comparisons fill the gaps (if A>B and B>C, we learn A>C).

3 How we work out the minimum number of annotators

This is the part that costs money, so it is worth explaining slowly. The goal is: collect just enough judgements so each word's score is stable, and not one paid response more.

Step 1 — What "precision" means here

Every word ends up with a score between 0 and 1 (roughly, "how often was this picked as strongest"). Because we only sample a limited number of people, that score has a margin of error, exactly like a political poll ("52% ± 3%"). That "±" part is the half-width, which we call h.

h = 0.15
"lean" precision — enough to rank words into clear tiers
h = 0.10
"strong" precision — enough to separate close neighbours

Step 2 — The polling formula

Just like a poll, the 95% margin of error shrinks with the square root of the number of independent observations M collected for a word. Using a conservative unit (standardized) variability per judgement — a deliberately safe over-estimate — the margin is:

h = 1.96 / √M

The 1.96 is the 95%-confidence multiplier. Turning this around to solve for how many observations per word we need to hit a target margin h:

M = (1.96 / h)2

Step 3 — From observations to people

Here is the key money-saving insight: each participant does not give us one observation per word — they give us r of them (they see each word r times). So the number of people we need is the number of observations divided by r:

N = M / r = (1.96 / h)2 / r
In plain words

Want more precision (smaller h) → need more people. Design that shows each word more often (bigger r) → need fewer people. That is why S3, with r = 9, needs the fewest annotators even though it has the most words.

Why these are minimums, not guesses

The formula deliberately assumes conservative (unit) variability and 95% confidence, so real precision is usually a little better than promised. We treat these as a floor and confirm with the reliability checks in the plan below rather than paying for more people up front.

4 Cost-aware recruitment plan

Rather than committing to the full "strong" numbers immediately, we start lean and only top up surveys that actually need it. This minimizes spend because most surveys stabilize early.

Wave 1 targets

S1 = 50 · S2 = 50 · S3 = 30 · S4 = 40 · S5 = 50 participants.

  • After Wave 1, run the checks below on each survey.
  • Add +15 participants only to surveys that fail.
  • Repeat until each survey passes twice in a row, then stop paying for that survey.

Stopping rule (pass both, twice in a row)

  • Split-half reliability ≥ 0.75 (prefer 0.80+): split responses in two random halves; the two rankings should strongly agree.
  • Top/bottom quartile overlap ≥ 85%: the strongest and weakest words stay the same as more data comes in.

5 Step-by-step pipeline from here

  1. Freeze the design. Tag the current S1–S5 CSVs as design_v1 so the item lists and blocks cannot drift mid-study.
  2. Confirm data capture. Verify the live Apps Script logs every column: Form ID, Block ID, Survey Number, choices, and participant metadata.
  3. Pilot QA (5–10 per survey). Check rows land in the sheet, block counts match, and sentences render correctly.
  4. Run Wave 1. Recruit the lean targets above.
  5. Checkpoint every +15. Recompute reliability and rank stability per survey.
  6. Selective top-up. Add participants only to surveys still failing (usually S1/S2 first, since they are near-balanced).
  7. Finalize scores. Produce BW-normalized scores plus a model-based estimate, and report each word's score with its uncertainty.
