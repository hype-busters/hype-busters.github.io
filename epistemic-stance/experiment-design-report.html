Epistemic Stance Survey — Design & Recruitment

How the study is built and how many participants it needs. This document explains what we are measuring, the question format, the five-survey split, and — step by step, in plain terms — exactly how we arrive at the minimum number of paid annotators.

What We Measure

We rank English words and phrases by how strongly they signal an epistemic stance — how certain, evidence-based, or committed a claim sounds. "This proves" is a far stronger stance than "this might suggest".
Why Best-Worst Scaling, not a rating scale Asking people to rate each word on a 1–7 scale is noisy — everyone uses the scale differently. Instead we show a few words in context and ask which is strongest and which is weakest. This forces a direct comparison, is easier to answer, and yields cleaner, more reliable rankings from fewer judgements.

Words are grouped into five surveys, one per stance category, so participants only compare words that genuinely belong together.

The Question Format

Each question is a block: four sentences that differ only by the stance word. The participant picks the strongest and the weakest of the four.
Why four items per block is efficient Picking a best and a worst from four options settles 5 of the 6 possible pairwise comparisons in that block at once. That is what makes BWS so economical — a handful of blocks implies a large number of comparisons.
v

k = 4

b
number of blocks (questions) in the survey
r

These four numbers are tied together by a simple accounting identity: the total item-slots shown is b × k, and when every item appears equally that equals v × r. So b × 4 = v × r.

The Five Surveys

Surveys 3–5 use a strict BIBD (Balanced Incomplete Block Design) — every pair of words is compared exactly once, the gold standard. Surveys 1–2 have too many words for a strict design to stay cheap, so they use a near-balanced design that still connects every word into one comparison network.

What "fully connected" means Even in S1/S2, where not every pair is compared directly, there are no isolated words — every word can still be ranked against every other through indirect chains (if A > B and B > C, we learn A > C).
Under review — PI feedback (Jul 2026) Two changes are prepared and staged (not yet live): (1) S3 → near-balanced to reduce fatigue — 35 blocks with each item seen 5×, instead of 63 blocks / 9×; and (2) optionally adding 2 anchor items per survey (one low, one high) to pin the ends of each scale and act as attention checks. Note that adding anchors shifts every survey to v ≡ 0 (mod 3), which makes a strict BIBD impossible — so all five would then use the near-balanced design.

How Many Annotators — Sized Empirically, Not by Formula

This is the part that costs money. Instead of fixing the number in advance with a sample-size formula, we begin with a reasonable sample, run it, and add participants only until the resulting scale is demonstrably stable.
Why a polling / proportion formula does not fit BWS A binomial margin-of-error formula — the "±3%" of opinion polls — assumes independent yes/no answers to one fixed question. Our task differs on three counts: (1) each judgement is a choice among four alternatives, not a binary; (2) an item's score is relational, depending on which other items it is shown with, so there is no single fixed proportion to estimate; and (3) an item's repeated appearances within one participant are not independent, so they cannot be treated as separate observations. A fixed a-priori N is therefore not defensible for this design.
The defensible approach — measure scale stability We judge sample size by repeated split-half reliability: randomly split the collected participants into two halves, score every item from each half independently, and correlate the two score sets — averaged over many random splits. As participants are added the correlation rises and then plateaus; we stop once it is high and stable. This is the standard validation used in the BWS literature (e.g. Kiritchenko & Mohammad, 2016/2017).
  • 1

    Run a modest starting sample

    Collect a reasonable first batch per survey (see the recruitment plan below) rather than a formula-derived target.

  • 2

    Measure split-half reliability

    Compute the average correlation between item scores from two random halves of participants, over many random splits.

  • 3

    Top up only if needed

    If a survey is below target, add a small increment of participants and recompute — repeat until the value plateaus.

  • 4

    Stop when stable

    Stop paying for a survey once reliability is high and the last increment barely moved it.

Target A split-half reliability of ≥ 0.80 that has plateaued (the last top-up barely changed it) is a strong, widely-accepted bar for a stable BWS scale. This replaces any fixed "N per survey": the data tells us when to stop.

Cost-Aware Recruitment Plan

We start with a modest sample per survey and top up only those that have not yet stabilised. Most surveys plateau early, so this minimises spend.

Pipeline From Here

Seven steps from the current frozen design to final published scores.
  • 1

    Freeze the design

    Tag the current S1–S5 CSVs as design_v1 so item lists and blocks cannot drift mid-study.

  • 2

    Confirm data capture

    Verify the live Apps Script logs every column: Form ID, Block ID, Survey Number, choices, and participant metadata.

  • 3

    Pilot QA — 5–10 per survey

    Check rows land in the sheet, block counts match, and sentences render correctly.

  • 4

    Run Wave 1

    Recruit the lean targets above (S1=50, S2=50, S3=30, S4=40, S5=50).

  • 5

    Checkpoint every +15

    Recompute split-half reliability and rank stability per survey.

  • 6

    Selective top-up

    Add participants only to surveys still failing — usually S1/S2 first, since they are near-balanced.

  • 7

    Finalise scores

    Produce BW-normalised scores plus a model-based estimate, and report each word's score with its uncertainty.
